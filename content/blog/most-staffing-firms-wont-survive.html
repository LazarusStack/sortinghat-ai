The staffing industry has always had a large middle: generalist firms of twenty to two hundred people, competing on speed and relationships across several verticals. That position worked because delivery capacity scaled with headcount, which meant size bought something. It no longer does, and a position that depends on an advantage that has disappeared is not a position.

Last reviewed August 2026. Structural argument, informed by Sortinghat delivery data and market observation.

Key takeaways

  • The generalist middle is the exposed position. Squeezed by specialists who know more and by scale operators who cost less.
  • Size stopped buying throughput. A small team with good tooling delivers volume that used to need a floor of recruiters.
  • Speed stopped being a differentiator. When everyone can submit in hours, submitting in hours is the entry requirement.
  • The survivors are smaller and more specialised, or much larger. Both ends are defensible. The middle is not.

What the middle was actually selling

A mid-sized generalist firm sold capacity and coverage. It could work more roles than a boutique and it knew the client better than a large operator with a call centre.

Both halves of that depended on headcount being the delivery constraint. Working more roles required more recruiters, so a firm with forty could do things a firm with six could not, and that was the moat.

When a filtered screening day produces around ten submission-ready profiles from roughly 300 contacts, the six-person firm delivers what the forty-person firm delivered. The moat did not narrow; it stopped being a moat.

Squeezed from both directions

From below, by specialists. A firm that owns one vertical knows the market better, has a deeper database in it, and can qualify a brief better than the client wrote it. That is a knowledge position and tooling does not erode it.

From above, by scale. Large operators have back office, balance sheet, geographic coverage and the ability to sit on enterprise panels. That is an infrastructure position and it also holds.

The middle has neither. It knows less than the specialist and costs more per unit than the scale operator, and it used to compensate with speed. Speed is now free.

Why this is not primarily an AI argument

Worth separating, because AI is a convenient explanation for a slower structural change.

Fee compression predates the current technology. Client internal talent teams have been getting better for a decade. Vendor management programmes have been standardising suppliers and squeezing margin for longer than that, as covered in VMS margin compression.

What the technology did was remove the last differentiator the middle had. It accelerated something that was already happening rather than causing it, which matters because the response is strategic rather than technological.

What survival actually looks like

Three routes, and they are genuinely different businesses.

Become the specialist. Pick one vertical, go deep enough that a client learns something from every conversation, and decline work outside it. The declining is the hard part and it is the credibility mechanism.

Become infrastructure. Build back office, capital and coverage that support volume at low margin. Capital intensive and not available to most.

Sell. A legitimate answer, and one worth planning three years ahead of, because the things that raise a valuation take that long. Covered in what staffing firms sell for.

Staying in the middle and working harder is the fourth option and it is the one most firms will choose.

Why this prediction might be wrong

Two honest counterarguments.

The industry has absorbed this before. Job boards, LinkedIn, applicant tracking systems and programmatic advertising were each going to eliminate agencies. Each removed work and the industry grew, because hiring volume grew faster than efficiency.

Relationships are stickier than models predict. Clients stay with suppliers they trust for longer than any efficiency argument suggests, and a mid-sized firm with genuine relationships can persist well past the point at which its position stopped being structurally defensible.

Both are real. Neither changes the direction, and a firm relying on the second is relying on inertia rather than on a plan.

Five checks worth running on your own desk

Everything above is context. These five turn it into something you can act on, and each takes under an hour.

1. Where did your last twenty placements come from?

Split them by source: your own database, a job portal, a referral, outbound sourcing. Most firms are surprised by how much they paid a portal for people already in their records. An audit of one firm's 3 million candidates found roughly 80 percent were real people whose records had simply gone out of date, covered in the database-first audit.

2. What is your actual time from brief to shortlist?

Measured from when the client briefed you, not from when the requisition was created in your system. In contingency work the firm that submits first usually gets paid, so this is a direct predictor of fill rate. Whether that bet still works is a separate question.

3. What share of gross profit sits with your largest client?

Concentration is the most common structural weakness in a staffing firm and the one founders notice last, usually when the account moves. It is also the single biggest discount on a valuation.

4. What is your response rate by channel?

Against your own baseline rather than a published benchmark. On strong roles we see roughly 40 responses per 100 contacted by phone, 25 on WhatsApp and 12 to 15 by email, broken down in our channel benchmarks. The ordering surprises teams who assumed messaging had replaced calling.

5. How many roles can one recruiter genuinely carry?

Not how many they are assigned, how many they can work properly. A recruiter running manual screening manages around 40 calls a day and finds three or four people worth submitting. That is the ceiling, and every growth plan sits on top of it. Filtering before dialling changes the arithmetic.

How to turn this into a client conversation

Market analysis has one practical use for a staffing firm, and it is not internal strategy. A founder who can explain the market to a client sounds different from every other agency in the inbox.

Lead with the market, not the capability deck

A hiring manager already knows what an agency does. What they do not have is a clear picture of their own talent market: who is hiring, what is being paid, how long roles are taking, where the supply sits. An agency that opens with that is a useful contact. One that opens with a process slide is a vendor.

Bring one number they cannot get elsewhere

Published sector data from NASSCOM or Staffing Industry Analysts is available to everyone, which makes it table stakes. What clients cannot get is what your own desk sees: your response rates by channel, your notice-period reality against what candidates state, your time from brief to shortlist. Even one such number, honestly caveated, changes the meeting.

Use it to reframe the brief rather than to win the pitch

The highest-value moment in a client conversation is telling them something about their own requirement they had not considered. That the band is below market. That the skill combination exists in forty people nationally. That the location constraint is costing six weeks. That earns exclusivity more reliably than any commercial concession, and it depends on defining the role properly before the search starts.

Log what the client tells you back

Client reactions are the most valuable input your firm collects and the least frequently recorded. What they pushed back on, what surprised them, what they revealed about the approval chain. If that lives in one recruiter's memory rather than on the client record, it leaves when they do, which is the argument for automatic capture.

Three mistakes firms make reading market data

Numbers get quoted in strategy decks and then change nothing. Three reasons that happens.

Treating a market size as an addressable market

A sector employing millions is not a sector where millions of placements are available to agencies. Internal talent teams, direct applications and referrals take the majority of any mature hiring market. The agency-addressable share is ramp-ups, niche roles and volume spikes.

Confusing a growing market with an easy one

Fast growth attracts suppliers. A segment growing at thirty percent usually has more agencies chasing it than one growing at five, so fee pressure rises alongside demand. Growth tells you where to look, not where it will be comfortable.

Planning around the trend instead of the capability gap

The useful question is which growing segment you can serve better than the incumbent supplier. A firm with no history in a domain entering it because the chart looks good will lose to the firm that has placed there for six years, because a search is only as good as the database behind it.

What this changes on a staffing desk

Market analysis is only useful if it changes something on Monday. Four practical consequences.

Your prospect list is probably built from history

Most firms sell to the accounts they already know, so the client list reflects where demand was five years ago. Rebuilding it against current hiring activity rather than past relationships is unglamorous and usually the highest-return week a founder can spend. A CRM built for that makes it repeatable.

Speed matters more than it used to

A recruiter working manually manages around 40 calls a day and finds three or four people worth submitting. Filtering before dialling changes the arithmetic: a pool search on a volume role surfaces around 1,200 relevant profiles, narrows to a ranked 300 contacted across call, WhatsApp and email in parallel, and produces roughly 10 submission-ready profiles in hours.

Your own database is the cheapest source you have

Making that 80 percent findable took job portal spend from about $100 a role to $60. Portal integrations are also where duplicates enter if matching does not happen at the point of entry, which we cover in duplicate candidate records.

Definition beats effort

Most wasted submissions are a briefing failure that surfaced three weeks later, not a sourcing failure. Generating weighted evaluation criteria from the job description and agreeing them with the client before the search starts removes more waste than any increase in activity. The mechanics are in hiring scorecards.

Frequently asked questions

Why are mid-sized staffing firms under pressure?

Because their position depended on headcount being the delivery constraint. Size bought throughput, which is what let a forty-person firm do things a six-person firm could not. Tooling removed that advantage without replacing it with another.

Is AI causing staffing firms to fail?

It accelerated a change already underway. Fee compression, improving internal talent teams and vendor management programmes were squeezing the middle for a decade. The technology removed the last differentiator rather than creating the problem.

Which staffing firms are best positioned?

Specialists who own a vertical deeply enough that clients learn from every conversation, and large operators with back office, capital and geographic coverage. Both are defensible positions; the generalist middle is not.

Can a mid-sized agency become a specialist?

Yes, and the hard part is declining work outside the chosen vertical. That refusal is the credibility mechanism, and it is what most firms cannot afford to do, which is precisely why it works for those who can.

Has technology threatened staffing before?

Repeatedly. Job boards, LinkedIn, applicant tracking systems and programmatic advertising were each expected to eliminate agencies. Each removed some work while the industry grew, because hiring volume grew faster than efficiency did.

Should a staffing firm owner consider selling?

It is a legitimate strategic answer and it needs roughly three years of preparation, because reducing client concentration, moving relationships from individuals to the firm and producing clean data all take that long to show in the numbers.

The question to answer honestly

Write down, in one sentence, why a client chooses your firm over the other three on the panel. If the sentence is about speed, service or relationships, every competitor would write the same one.

If you cannot write a different sentence, that is the strategic problem, and no amount of operational improvement addresses it.
