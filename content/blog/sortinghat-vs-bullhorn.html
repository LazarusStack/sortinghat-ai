Where the two platforms actually differ

Search and matching

Bullhorn. Core search is keyword and Boolean, and it holds up at scale on large databases. Semantic and natural-language search exist through the Amplify Search & Match add-on built on Textkernel and SourceBreaker, which is an optional premium product rather than part of the base ATS.

Sortinghat. Search is semantic from the first day. Describe the person in a sentence, including conditions that are judgements rather than keywords, such as institution tier or company stage, and get a ranked result with the reasoning visible. No Boolean training and no second contract.

Bottom line: Bullhorn sells the AI search as a separately quoted upgrade. With Sortinghat it is the starting point.

Screening and qualification

Bullhorn. Screening is a recruiter activity. The platform tracks it; it does not do it. A recruiter running a manual calling day manages around 40 calls and finds three or four people worth submitting.

Sortinghat. AI voice and video screening run inside the platform against the role's weighted criteria. A filtered shortlist of roughly 300 is contacted across call, WhatsApp and email in parallel, producing around 10 submission-ready profiles in hours, with notice period, compensation and CV claims already verified.

Bottom line: This is the largest single difference between the two platforms, and it is the one that shows up in placements rather than in a feature list.

Data quality and record upkeep

Bullhorn. Bullhorn stores data at very large scale, and keeping it current is the recruiter's job. Records go stale unless somebody updates them, and enrichment means another add-on or a marketplace tool.

Sortinghat. Records maintain themselves as the team works. Calls, emails and WhatsApp threads attach to the right person automatically, and the AI notetaker turns conversations into structured fields rather than transcripts nobody reads.

Bottom line: Bullhorn data is only as fresh as the last manual update. Sortinghat records stay current because working the desk is what updates them.

Communication channels

Bullhorn. Email is native from Core up. Beyond that, each channel is a product: SMS needs the Messaging add-on, sequencing needs Bullhorn Automation, and WhatsApp runs through marketplace partners rather than natively.

Sortinghat. Call, email, WhatsApp and LinkedIn all thread onto one timeline against the person, and multichannel campaigns run natively. On an India desk this is decisive, because WhatsApp is where candidates actually reply.

Bottom line: Bullhorn sells communication channel by channel. Sortinghat keeps every channel on the record with the context attached.

Where Bullhorn is still a good choice

Not every firm should switch, and these are the cases where Bullhorn remains the better answer.

  • You need the specific marketplace partner you already run. If a niche point solution is embedded in your operation and only integrates with Bullhorn, that is a real constraint worth weighing.

The three questions that actually decide this

Comparison tables inform the decision. They rarely make it. Three questions do, and all three are about your firm rather than either platform.

What is capping your revenue?

Not what is annoying. What is capping revenue. If your recruiters cannot carry more roles, the answer is throughput, and throughput comes from removing manual sourcing and screening rather than from better record keeping. Firms routinely buy for the annoyance and change nothing.

What happens to the team in week one?

The most common failure in this category is not a bad platform. It is a good platform nobody switched to. Ask both vendors what happens in week one, week four and week twelve, who does the migration, and what it costs. A vague answer is the answer.

What does the all-in number look like at year three?

Licence plus implementation plus migration plus every module you have to add to reach a working setup plus renewal escalation. Then subtract what the platform removes from recruiter hours and job portal spend. Vendors who will only discuss the per-seat headline are telling you something about the other five lines.

What to test before you decide

Five things that separate a real evaluation from a feature tour. Run them on both platforms with your own data, not the vendor's demo database.

  1. Search your own database in plain English. Describe someone you actually placed and see whether they appear in the top twenty.
  2. Open a candidate score and ask why. If the answer is a number with no reasoning behind it, your recruiters will ignore the ranking.
  3. Look at one candidate contacted on three channels. One timeline, or three separate logs?
  4. Ask what the all-in price is, including every module you would actually license. Then ask what it renews at.
  5. Ask for the migration plan in writing, with anything that will not carry across named explicitly.

What a migration should actually include

Migration is the reason most firms stay on a platform they outgrew, so it is worth knowing what good looks like before you ask any vendor about it.

Notes, calls, attachments and submission history. These are the records most migrations leave behind and the ones that make a database worth keeping. Ask explicitly rather than assuming, because "we migrate your data" and "we migrate your history" are different promises.

Field mapping done by the vendor. An export file and a spreadsheet is not a migration. Somebody has to decide what maps where, and it should not be your operations lead in the evenings.

A validation environment before go-live. Your team should review the migrated data and sign it off, rather than discovering gaps in week three when a client asks about a candidate.
