An offshore recruiting team can source, screen and prepare submissions to a standard indistinguishable from an onshore one. Where offshore delivery breaks is the handoff: information lost between the person who took the brief and the person doing the work, and again between the person doing the work and the person presenting it. The delivery model is essentially a decision about how many handoffs to accept.

The three models

Follow the sun. The client briefs at the end of their day, work happens overnight, output waits in the morning. Maximum time efficiency, maximum handoff risk, and it only works if the offshore team can complete a full cycle without asking a question.

Overlapping hours. The offshore team covers part of the client's working day, allowing real-time contact. Fewer handoffs, better quality, less time efficiency. This is the natural model for India into the Gulf and into Australia, per selling into Australia.

Fully embedded. The offshore team works the client's hours entirely. Highest quality, highest human cost, and it burns people out if run indefinitely without rotation.

Why handoffs are the failure point

The information that decides most rejections was never written down. It was in the tone of the briefing conversation, in a passing remark about the last person who failed, or in something the hiring manager did not think to mention.

Onshore, one person holds that context. Offshore with a handoff, somebody has to write it down, and what gets written is the requirement rather than the nuance.

The corrective is structural rather than motivational: capture the brief as weighted criteria agreed with the client at intake, so that what matters is explicit rather than remembered, per hiring scorecards.

Why overlap beats headcount

Firms solving offshore delivery problems usually add people. Two hours of genuine overlap solves more than two additional recruiters.

In that window a question gets answered, a brief gets clarified, and a shortlist gets discussed rather than sent. That single conversation removes an entire cycle of wrong-direction work, which no amount of additional capacity recovers.

The practical implication is that a smaller team with real overlap outperforms a larger team without it, and it is cheaper. Firms staffing offshore delivery should design the overlap first and the headcount second.

What travels well and what does not

Travels well: sourcing, database work, screening against defined criteria, scheduling, submission preparation, reporting. All process work with a clear standard, and all of it improves with automation.

Travels badly: persuading a reluctant senior candidate, reading a hiring manager's unstated concern, and managing an offer through a counter-offer.

The firms that do this well split explicitly on that line and say so to clients. The ones that claim full-service offshore delivery get tested on the second list, usually on a role that matters.

SLAs that actually mean something

Most offshore delivery SLAs measure activity, which is easy to hit and tells the client nothing.

Four that are worth committing to. Time from brief to first submission. Submission-to-interview ratio, which measures whether your judgement matches theirs. Response time within stated hours. And time to escalate when something is stuck, which is the one that builds trust.

Committing to profiles sourced per day is a commitment to look busy, and experienced clients read it that way.

How automation changes the model

The traditional argument for follow-the-sun was that offshore work took a full night to produce anything.

When a filtered screening day produces roughly ten submission-ready profiles in hours rather than days, the offshore team completes a cycle inside the overlap window. That makes overlapping hours viable where follow-the-sun was previously the only option, which removes handoffs and improves quality at the same time.

The practical consequence: firms designing offshore delivery around throughput can choose a better model than firms designing around headcount, per what one screening day produces.

Frequently asked questions

What is the main risk in offshore recruitment delivery?

Handoffs rather than sourcing quality. The information that decides most rejections is never written down, so it is lost between the person who took the brief and the person doing the work.

What offshore delivery models exist?

Follow the sun with an overnight cycle, overlapping hours allowing real-time contact during part of the client's day, and fully embedded where the offshore team works the client's hours entirely.

Is overlap or headcount more important in offshore delivery?

The design decision to make first

Decide the overlap window before deciding the headcount. Two hours of genuine real-time contact with the client changes delivery quality more than any additional recruiter you could add to a fully asynchronous team.

Then check whether your process can complete a cycle inside that window. If it cannot, that is the constraint rather than the time zone.
